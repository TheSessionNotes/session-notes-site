Bobby Borg is a drummer turned teacher. He studied at Berklee College of Music, then recorded and toured as a signed artist with Atlantic Records before moving to the business side of music. He went back to school, earned a master's degree from USC, and now teaches at USC's Thornton School of Music. He has also written several books that help musicians understand marketing, business, and money, and he runs a YouTube channel full of advice for people trying to make a living in music.

I wanted to talk to Bobby because he has done the exact switch I'm thinking about: growing up behind a drum kit and then moving toward the business side. I'd watched a lot of his videos and read parts of his books before we talked on Zoom, and I came away with more to think about than I expected.

**You started out chasing a career as a drummer and later crossed over into business. What stands out to you about that journey?**

When I was pursuing a career as a drummer, people sort of respected you because you went for it. You're a musician, you went up against the odds, and a lot of people wouldn't even dare go after something like that. It's like going, "I'm going to be a professional snowboarder." It's a shot in the dark.

Most people go to school and they want to get out and, at 23, have the 90 or 100 or 120,000 dollar job. Now they're getting their own cars and their own apartments and going on their own trips, and they feel like an adult. And here you are, taking all these massive risks, and in some cases you're sleeping on your friends' floors. So in one respect, people are respecting you for that. But on the other end, you're kind of weird. You're that weird, crazy guy that went for his dreams.

And then when you cross over into the business side, you're respected for that, but at the same time you're not as cool anymore, or you're not as current, or you're not as much of an insider. It's almost like this double standard.

People always like to frame you in their mind as it is comfortable to them. What people normally are doing is projecting. They're projecting their own insecurities on you, and you have to totally ignore those things. I noticed how those projections completely flipped. It was like night and day. And it just makes you realize to never let other people dictate what it is you do, because normally they are not being honest with you. They're just projecting their own fears on you.

**In one of your videos, you talk about coming in second on a lot of auditions. How can a young musician tell when it's time to stop waiting to get picked and start building their own thing?**

At first it sounds like a negative video, but it ends up being a positive one. I think it's when you get fed up and you get to a point where you're like, "I am so convinced I want to do this, I'm so convinced I'm good enough to do this, I'm so convinced that I'm putting in the time and the effort to earn it. But for some weird reason, I am not getting it." I'm getting in the door, but in the final decision, I'm not getting it. That basically means I have what it takes, but there's one small thing that's missing.

So what I did is I searched for that one small thing, and I found an answer that made sense to me. Then I said, "Okay, let's change that. Let's go in and fix that problem."

The important thing is always to be looking for answers, and always be willing to blame yourself, not other people or the circumstances. Some people could easily go, "Something's wrong with the world, something's wrong with the system, life isn't fair." I never did that. I was like, "All right, this is awesome. I'm getting in on some huge auditions. Why am I not getting them?" So I looked in the mirror and said, "Let's analyze this." I found something that made sense, and that's when I decided to go off and do my own thing.

**I grew up playing drums and I'm thinking about going into the business side. What kind of edge does thinking like a musician give you?**

When you're in the music business and you understand both sides, it gives you a little bit of an edge. Not only are you adept in business, but you understand music and what it is that you're selling, ultimately. You understand the mindset of the musician, what it is to be passionate, to have a desire to connect with people, to make art. At the same time, you understand the principles of business, which a lot of artists don't.

We could flip the question and ask musicians what edge they'd have if they understood the business. When you're a musician, you put a lot of heart into it and you don't put enough mind into it. It sounds really cool when musicians make it without putting any mind into it, because then they can say, "I just did what I wanted to do. I didn't make music for other people. I made music for myself." But do you know how many millions of musicians are like that and are broke? So I think a balance of both can be really beneficial. It's great that you think something could sell, but it would also be good if you understood why.

We used to joke about the suits versus the rockers, and that the suits probably couldn't hit a middle C on a piano if you pointed at it. I don't think that's good. I think you should understand music a little bit. If you understand both sides, you'll be more well-rounded in how you connect with musicians. You can go into a studio and the musicians aren't going to be talking behind your back, because you know your stuff.

**So having done both sides makes a difference?**

Think about football commentators. When you have a quarterback that used to be a star, and he won seven Super Bowls, and now he's talking about it, you tend to take his opinion a little more seriously than an analyst who never played. It's not that they can't do a good job. It's just a little different when someone actually played the game.

I started off as a true musician. I was practicing 10, 12, 14 hours a day. I studied with the world's greatest drummers in New York and went to Berklee College of Music. When I got out of school, I was willing to sleep on couches. I was the real thing. And then I crossed over and paid the same dues on the other side. I took over 52 business classes at UCLA. I have an undergrad, a master's, and a doctorate almost. I'm maybe a year away from a doctorate.

But when I was a musician, the business people didn't take me seriously because I wasn't a business person. And on the business side, some people look at you like, "Oh, you're just a musician turned business guy. What do you know?" Then there are others who go, "Well, now you're not a musician anymore, so what do you know about music?" You can never please everyone. That goes back to the projecting thing. You just have to do what you do.

**High schools don't really teach personal finance. What habits should someone my age start building now?**

One of the problems with personal finance and starting early is the way the human mind works, if you study developmental psychology. When you're young, and I say this respectfully, there are parts of your brain that haven't developed yet. You're not 100% thinking about consequences. A lot of times, young people just want to be ballers. The bigger car, the bigger house, the bigger vacations, the bigger watch they can show all their friends, and all the pictures on Instagram. Usually, until a later age, it doesn't click.

Somebody is really going to have to have it together to go, "At age 20, I'm going to start putting money away in an index fund and let it grow, so by the time I'm 50 I can be a tri-millionaire." When you're 20, 50 sounds ancient. You never think of yourself as being retired, and when you think of retired, you think of some old guy in goofy pants playing golf.

So you almost have to take yourself outside of yourself to get that mindset. Maybe use your parents or your uncle or other people who are successful, and go, "I'm going to start doing that now." In other words, be more influenced by older people than by younger people. One of the reasons I did a lot of the things I did is because my number one influence was my dad, and my drum teacher. These were 50- and 60-year-old people. I was not influenced by my friends. I'd look at my uncles and go, "Wow, look how successful he is. Look how much respect he gets. What did he do? That's what I'm going to do," at age 12.

**As drummers, we see people doing crazy stuff with their sticks, but the really impressive players are the ones who stay perfectly in the pocket. How does that connect to how drummers can approach the music business?**

What young kid doesn't want to be back there flashing off, doing fast drum solos and impressing everybody? Every young kid wants to do that. It's not until you get a little older and more mature that you realize a lot of times it's about having a pocket, being solid, and playing for the song. Because at the end of the day, we're not selling drum solos. We're selling songs. You start thinking more like a business person when you get older, and you start thinking about what you shouldn't play more than what you can play.

But now I'm going to completely destroy that answer with a recent interview I saw with Stewart Copeland, the drummer for The Police. He said there's this idea that drummers are supposed to play for the song, and he goes, "No. Songwriters play for the song. I'm a drummer. I play for the drummers." He loves intricate rhythms, cool fills, technique, chops. The problem they had was that Sting wanted him to play for the song, and he'd throw in crazy, complicated fills and Sting would turn around and go, "Why are you doing that?" Ultimately they met in the middle, and that's what you hear on the records.

So the question is: do you want to be a drummer, or do you want to be the member of a successful band because you're more concerned with selling songs? Or a session player who helps other people create hit albums because you know how to play the song? Are you a songer instead of a drummer? Personally, if you want to make a living, you should probably be a songer. There are the people who listen to songs, and there are the people who listen to drums. It's a smaller market. It's not that you couldn't be successful as a drummer. You could write drum books, be a drum hero. But my problem was I wanted to be a drummer in a drummer band. I wanted to be in Tool. I wanted to be in Led Zeppelin. I wanted to be in Rush. And most people aren't Rush.

I trained more to be a drummer, and I didn't want to be a songer. When I finally realized I had to be one, I was almost depressed. I almost wanted to quit. But then I said, "You know what? Every job sucks a little bit." If you're a lawyer, you have to deal with different clients. If you're a doctor, you deal with the administration and the headaches of the hospital. So I figured I might as well do something I love and deal with some of the hassle.

**Any last advice for someone my age?**

When you're younger, you're going through so much: growing pains, identity crisis, trying to find your independence, trying to prove to people that you can succeed. And there are so many stupid things you could do to get yourself in trouble. This is going to sound like a total old-fart answer, but if you can latch onto some mentors, your parents included, and really trust that, "Look, they're 20 or 30 years ahead of me. They must have done something right." Listening to an older mentor is not a bad idea. On the other hand, I always like to look at both sides. Someone who's older shouldn't be stuck in their ways either. They should be open-minded to new ideas, too.

And you should really think about your strengths. Maybe use your blog and your questions as a potential career move. Maybe you have a knack for being an innovator, for starting your own business, for being creative. Most high school kids just don't start a blog and start reaching out to people to interview them. Maybe there's something there.
