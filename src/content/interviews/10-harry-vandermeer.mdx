Harry Van der Meer is 22, from Northern California, and currently based in Boston. He's a bass player and a marching percussionist — and for the last four years he was seriously both at once, flying between Berklee College of Music and drum corps tour while trying to be excellent at two very different instruments.

He's now aged out of drum corps, graduated from Berklee, and playing bass in the rock band Sophie's Body, who just put out their debut album and are moving toward New York. This conversation is about doing two things at once, what it costs, and why he eventually put his weight behind the one that doesn't end at 21.

**Let's start at the beginning. How did you get into music?**

My mom and dad are gymnasts — they own a small business teaching gymnastics. They had a former student who went off to Berklee in Boston, and he came back to Petaluma and started teaching lessons. I was at the facility while my parents were working all the time, so they put me in private lessons basically so I'd have something to do other than twiddle my thumbs.

That's around age seven. I started on piano and took lessons weekly or biweekly for a long time. I thought it was cool, but I wasn't in love with it yet. Eventually I hopped around instruments and found my way to bass, and I was a little more into that. I vividly remember being in the car one day and saying, "Mom, music lessons with Brian are really cool — can I do more of them?" That's Brian Dolan, my teacher. A very special man, very influential to me.

That got the ball rolling. For a year or two I was just developing skills with no real reason for it. Then I joined my middle school jazz band, and that went really well.

**How did you end up on percussion, then?**

This is where it turned. I wanted to do jazz band in high school, but the rule was you couldn't do it unless you also did concert band — and I didn't want to do that, so I said no. My middle school teacher told me I'd really like it and that I should change my mind, and he emailed the guy for me.

So I show up with nothing ready or planned, and the director says, cool, welcome — what instrument do you want to play? And then somebody — my brother's girlfriend at the time — literally dragged me to the percussion closet and said, "You're going to do percussion." I said, alright, sure.

From there the rest is history. I had a natural touch with drums, partly because Brian had taught me a bit of drum set. But with marching percussion I think I just had a knack for it. Obviously I worked my butt off too. It wasn't that I wanted to grind and be the best and achieve my dreams — I just couldn't drop those drumsticks. Ask anybody from high school and they'll tell you: if you're looking for him, he's probably drumming. It'd be true. I'd be in the practice room.

**How did Berklee happen?**

COVID hit, and I was still drumming and posting on Instagram, but I was also still playing bass in jazz band. It got to the point where future plans had to be questioned. I wasn't done with bass or with other kinds of music.

My buddy Cody auditioned for Berklee and got in — he didn't end up going, he's at USC and just graduated — but it inspired me to take college more seriously, because up to then I didn't really care.

I made B Corps in December of 2021, and then in February I took a last-minute shot at Berklee, honestly just for the hell of it, because I wasn't really thinking about anywhere else. I got in with a pretty decent scholarship, and I thought, alright, let's do it.

So the past four years I've been balancing being a marching percussionist while also trying to become a high-level Berklee bass player. Flying back and forth between Boston and Laramie, Wyoming, doing tour, all of it. It's been a crazy four years.

**Those are basically two full-time jobs. How did you manage it?**

Honestly, I'm only just getting over the hill with it. I can look back on it now because I've aged out — I'm not eligible for another indoor season, and I'm not going to do it.

And I kind of want to say that I wasn't really managing it. It just kind of sucked.

I'm not saying I was miserable for four years. But I'd be lying if I said there wasn't a feeling of sacrifice for the marching arts, because I'm really invested in the bass stuff and really trying to work on my future — something that's going to last.

That's a big reason I stuck with bass, and it's what my parents were pushing for too. They'd say, this marching stuff is amazing, and you clearly have a good touch for it — but realistically, what does that career path look like? And it looks like teaching. There's nothing wrong with that. In fact there's everything right with it; teaching is amazing. But for me it didn't rub the right way.

<div class="statement">It puts this idea in your head that you peak at 21 and you're out of the game and you're washed. That's not how that works. That's not life.</div>

**Was it hard watching friends keep marching?**

I can't tell you how many times I'd be here in February grinding for school, and it's 11 degrees out, and I'm looking at my phone at my best friends. Eddie Morales — that guy's like my brother, we got contracted together and spent three summers at Devs. When he decided to go and made RCC for 2025, I was just like, bruh. I thought about all those possibilities, being around those people, because the tough part is you can't do it again once you're done.

The saving grace was that I was building and sustaining and preparing for a future with bass here — with a band and friends and other music that gives me the same amount of love and fulfillment, but doesn't have a time limit. It's sustainable in that sense, as a performer.

**So what's next? What are you excited about?**

Honestly, what I've been looking forward to most is stasis — being in the same spot. This is my first summer in Boston ever, and it's been so good to me. I've had my moments of post-age-out, post-college blues. But I haven't been alone with myself in a room for a while. If you've been on tour, you know exactly what I mean.

The band's going pretty well. Sophie's Body is a rock band, all original music, and we're really pushing ourselves. We've got a crazy gig coming up in September in Atlanta — a really big festival where we're technically on the same bill as Twenty One Pilots. We're at the bottom of a huge list, but it counts. We released three singles over the summer and our debut album is coming out September 4th.

**How did you meet the band?**

First couple weeks of freshman year, I walk into a room because I thought there was a jam happening — just wanting to make some music and meet somebody. I walk in on Sophie, who's the lead singer, guitarist, and songwriter, and Dylan, our drummer. They're rehearsing.

Sophie immediately goes, "This is a closed rehearsal, you need to leave right now." And I'm like, oh God, I'm so sorry. So I turn around — and they see the bass on my back. And it's, "Whoa whoa whoa, hang on. Where are you going?"

They let me plug in, we played some stuff, and that was that.

It wasn't intentional at first, it was casual. But eventually we spent enough time together that it got serious, because I think we all understood we had something to offer. Sophie's original music is undeniably really great, regardless of genre. We all subliminally understood that what we have is worth taking a shot at. Since then we've done a West Coast tour, a bunch of New England tours, hit the South, and we're going back this October.

We're all Berklee grads, actually. It's funny — sometimes we try not to mention it, because Berklee is amazing but there are stereotypes that can outlast you and give a certain first impression.

**How are you supporting yourself as a musician, especially with the move to New York?**

There are definitely other gigs I'm interested in. My dream scenario is that Sophie's Body goes triple platinum, we make it — and also that I'm getting called for other really cool high-profile gigs.

There's a scene around New York — the kind of music Tim Miller is making — that's really big and really cool. That's what I'm looking for. Being a touring musician is the dream.

I got a lot out of being around jazz players at Berklee. It's pretty much a jazz school, so all my best buddies were jazz musicians, and I got into fusion, R&B, and funk too. My senior year I auditioned for and got to lead the Prince Ensemble — a year-long directed study dedicated to Prince and his music and legacy. Most Berklee ensembles are pretty low-key; this one was a huge production, something you'd see touring around the world. Music like that, where I get to express myself and play with my friends outside the band, is a big part of it.

**Last one. What's your advice for musicians coming up who want to go down a path like yours?**

Trust your gut. There's a gut feeling with a lot of this stuff, and realistically, if you don't love what you're doing there's a good chance it won't work out the way you want.

I feel like I've had a different bass story than I've had a quad story. With quads, I wasn't concerned about success or status or where I'd march. All I cared about was when the next time I could get in my room and play on a practice pad was. That's it. I loved the feeling of the sticks in my hand, learning a new lick, sharing it with people I love online. It was never for anything else — I just loved it and was obsessed with it.

Any aspiring musician should find what that is for them and chase it. No matter where it goes, you're going to be in love with it — and realistically, when that happens, any success or opportunities will gravitate toward you. I guarantee it, because that's exactly what happened with quads. I just played and played and played, and then it almost felt like all of a sudden I was in the Blue Devils, if that makes sense.

That's what I'm discovering with bass right now too. I'm getting over more and more hills because I've been able to spend more time with it, and I'm really finding what I love and what I'm obsessed with. That's all you need. The rest will carry you there. I promise that for sure.
