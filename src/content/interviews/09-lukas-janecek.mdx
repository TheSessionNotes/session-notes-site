Lukas Janecek is a Grammy-winning guitarist based in Los Angeles, originally from the Czech Republic. He picked up a guitar at 16 or 17 — late, by most standards — and taught himself almost entirely from YouTube, in a country where he says it was hard to find the kind of players he wanted to learn from.

His path since has been anything but standard: Berklee College of Music in Boston, then a community college in East Hollywood, then USC Thornton. He'll tell you the community college stretch — the part that looks like a step backward on paper — was one of the best things that happened to him.

This conversation is about that road, the faith that runs underneath it, and a teacher who told him it was already too late.

<InstagramEmbed url="https://www.instagram.com/reel/DHsb1thOja5/" />

**Let's start at the beginning. How did you get into music?**

The way I got into music at all was kind of supernatural for me. I grew up in a church in the Czech Republic — we've always been a Christian family, so we were always going to church. I started late, in my late teens, 16 or 17.

This pastor from San Diego came to our church. I don't know why he came, but he did, and he basically prophesied over me — that he saw me playing guitar, that I'd be in the USA, and that I'd be successful at it. At that moment I was like, yeah, whatever. I didn't believe it. I listened to music like everyone does, but I'd never thought about playing an instrument.

Two or three weeks after that, I was at my cousin's place. He always played guitar. He put on "Hey Joe" by Jimi Hendrix on YouTube, and when I took the guitar into my hands — for my ears, at that moment, it was like, okay, I've got it. That same day I ordered my first electric guitar. I practiced for three months without even having an amp. Twelve hours a day, even more, locked in my bedroom. That was the start of my whole music life.

**Not many people know that part of your story.**

No, not many people do. I don't usually talk about it because it's a longer story — I usually just say it's God's way. But when I look back now, whatever we have prepared for us, God's way is better, even if we don't think so at the moment.

**How was the transition from church-centered music to mainstream recording?**

When I started playing it was mostly church music, learning that type of stuff. But I really liked blues, and then jazz — Jimi Hendrix, Stevie Ray Vaughan, Eric Clapton. Those are really important parts of my life. Then one day on YouTube I saw John Mayer on the recommended page, and that's how I got into the mainstream pop and R&B world.

So even though I grew up in church learning from church musicians, and that's my foundation, I developed the other kinds of music on my own at the same time. That part was pretty easy for me.

What was harder is that the music scene in the Czech Republic isn't really that good, honestly — the musicians just aren't at the level they are in the United States. Even though you can learn everything on YouTube, especially back then, ten years ago, you need to be surrounded by musicians and playing with them in real life. That was hard to get there. It's genuinely difficult to find great mainstream, pop, and R&B players in Czech.

**So the way you play now is mostly self-taught.**

Definitely, for sure.

And there's this thing from when I was 16 or 17 — older than most people are when they start an instrument. In Czech, and in Europe generally, there are elementary art schools for music you can enroll in while you're in school. I went there and they were kind of full, and the teacher told me some really bad things: that I was old, that it would have been better if I'd never even started, that I'd never achieve anything.

I remember it very vividly. I sat down on the stairs and I thought, I'm not going to cry — I'm going to go and show him what I can do.

I did want to have an education and a teacher or coach before going to the U.S. But that never happened. So it was mostly me, self-taught, just researching things and learning on my own.

**How did you first get to the U.S.?**

I came for the first time about one or two years after I started playing, when I was 17 or 18 — really young. I went for a summer, stayed two or two and a half months, and did music workshops. I even went to Bethel in Redding, California, for a summer camp, and played there.

After absorbing that atmosphere and being around those musicians, I got back to Europe and thought, I need to go to college in the United States. That's the place for me to be, and I felt like God wanted me there too.

**You're a person of faith, and mainstream recording is a very different world. Has that been difficult?**

I wouldn't say it affected me, because I've been pretty lucky to meet really good people in the business. Of course I've met people doing things that maybe aren't the true way of God. But I think I have good social skills, so with anyone I've met I've been able to handle it pretty easily. I've never had anything that caused a conflict.

Honestly, some of the people I've met who don't believe in God are really nice — even without that faith, they'd look at certain behavior and think, man, what you're doing is kind of too much. You hear about that stuff; it's on the news, it's everywhere. Some people are like that. But I've been lucky.

**What have been your favorite sessions or gigs?**

I wouldn't say there's one big one that was really memorable. It's more the little things — like being at Berklee in Boston with my friends at two, three, four in the morning, somebody brings great Chinese food, and we're recording. Those personal little moments had a bigger impact on me, because of the people I was with, than meeting some big name.

Recording with friends I love is no better than recording with Bruno Mars, or whoever. It's about how you feel and what kind of people you have around you. That's what it's all about.

**You went to Berklee, then LACC, then USC. That's an unusual path. What did each place give you?**

I went all around, honestly — probably one of the few people who did. The friendships I made at Berklee were more long-lasting and deeper than the ones at USC. I don't mean that in a wrong way, it's just how it was.

But what I learned at USC was on a more complex and challenging level. Berklee is still, I'd say, the best music school in the world, and they push you to do things you wouldn't necessarily want to do — especially as a guitar player, things like reading music. That pushes you in directions you wouldn't explore on your own, which challenges you more.

Then after Berklee I went to LACC, community college in Los Angeles, because it had been really hard with scholarships and everything.

**That's a big change. How did you handle the doubt?**

I don't want to put it down in any way, but of course everyone knows community college — 100% acceptance rate — versus the best school in the world. Naturally it was a big change.

But thank God, the music department and the teachers at LACC are actually excellent. If you look it up online it ranks pretty high, even above some private schools. When I was making that life-changing decision I was praying about it, looking at schools I'd need to go to in LA or San Francisco, and it came to me: it'll be LACC in East Hollywood.

I got there and the teachers really helped. It honestly helped me grow more than I ever expected. I'm super grateful for that. I believe it was the right choice from God at that moment.

Going from the best school in the world to a community college in LA — you do believe in God, but it's still scary. And then it turned out I made true friendships there, met some great people, and it worked out.

**What are your future plans?**

I'd really want to get on a big world tour with someone like Bruno Mars or John Mayer — really big artists I look up to and who inspire me. That's my biggest desire.

Beyond that, doing sessions with the best of the best in the industry and growing on my instrument. And probably writing my own stuff. Everybody knows I'm the worst singer ever, so it's hard — I'd need to do something instrumental, which is cool too. Solo guitar with a trio or my own band. Something original.

But the focus right now is the big tour. Personally, and I think for every musician, there's more joy in playing live in front of people. There's an energy in that presence. That's what I'd really try to get on.

**Last one. What's your advice for aspiring musicians trying to follow a path like yours?**

Be yourself, work hard, and never let anyone put you down or tell you that you can't do something — because you can do anything you put your mind to.

That's exactly why I want to put this message out there. What that teacher said to me when I was picking up the guitar — he didn't use those exact words, but it amounted to "it would be better if you'd never started." That hits hard. A lot of people would be turned down by that and stop.

But if I can go from a small country in Europe that almost nobody knows about, come to the United States, and play with some of the biggest names in the industry, then you can do anything too. Work hard, believe in yourself, and never quit. Just keep going.
