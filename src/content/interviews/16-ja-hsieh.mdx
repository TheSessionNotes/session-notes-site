Dr. Ja Hsieh is a percussionist and teacher in the San Francisco Bay Area. He grew up in Taiwan, where he first played timpani in his school band in fifth grade, and he later spent years as a full-time member of a professional percussion group that toured Europe. In 2008 he moved to the United States to study at the University of Washington. Since then he has played at Carnegie Hall, recorded an album of Bach's violin music on vibraphone, and taught at De Anza College. In 2016 he founded Cha Cha Percussion in Santa Clara, a program where kids and adults learn to play percussion together in groups, including ensembles for students with special needs.

Dr. Hsieh answered these questions in writing. I'm a drummer heading into college to study the music business, so I asked him how he got started, what touring was like, how he prepares for big performances, and what a career in percussion looks like week to week.

**Growing up in Taiwan, you studied classical percussion with Tzong-Ching Ju at TNUA and drum set with Rich Huang. What pulled you in first, and how did both shape you?**

I first became interested in percussion in fifth grade, when I played timpani in the school band. I was drawn to its big, powerful sound.

In high school, I was blown away by a percussion ensemble concert. I thought, "This is what I want to do." I wanted to make percussion my career and perform and tour with a percussion ensemble.

My main teacher in college was Jue-Wen Chang, who taught me classical percussion. Tzong-Ching Ju's influence was more about how I thought about music. I also became very interested in drum set during college and studied with Rich Huang. With him, I focused on tone and groove: how to bring more depth to my drum set playing and apply those ideas and techniques to percussion ensemble playing.

**Before moving to the US, you toured with the Ju Percussion Group and played with Les Percussions de Strasbourg and Amadinda. What was tour life like in those ensembles?**

I was a full-time member of the Ju Percussion Group, and we worked with Les Percussions de Strasbourg and Amadinda on collaborative projects.

On a ten-day tour in Europe, for example, we might spend the first three or four days in intensive rehearsals, followed by two or three concerts. Most of our time was spent going between rehearsal spaces and concert halls. If we had time after the performances, we would do some sightseeing and explore the city.

**In 2008 you came to the University of Washington on a Boeing Fellowship to study with Tom Collier. Why leave Taiwan for a DMA, and how did he shape your vibraphone work?**

Good question! I wanted to keep growing as a musician. I had never studied abroad, and I wanted to experience student life in another country, learn about a different culture, and see how that could influence my music. After working full-time in a percussion ensemble, it felt like a chance to become a student again, to step back, learn, and be open to new ideas.

Tom Collier is well known as a jazz vibraphonist, and I was curious about his approach to the instrument and how he interpreted music. I enjoy hearing how other musicians think, but I also come back to asking myself what makes my own playing different. Studying with him was a chance to explore that and keep looking for repertoire that felt right for me.

**You've played Bartók's *Sonata for Two Pianos and Percussion* at Carnegie Hall and Michael Daugherty's timpani concerto *Raise the Roof*. How do you prepare for shows like those?**

In the Bartók, I played the timpani part, which has a major role in the piece. I listened to a lot of recordings to understand what the other players were doing, because the four parts are closely connected. I paid particular attention to the piano melodies and where I could support them and bring out the music.

For *Raise the Roof*, I studied guaguancó rhythms on congas and explored how to play them on timpani. I also listened to Gregorian chant and tried to recreate that sense of sound echoing through a church. In the cadenza, I really pushed the pace and built the tension to make it as exciting as possible.

So listening is a big part of my preparation, but I also bring a lot of my own imagination into the performance.

**On *Bach on Vibraphone*, you became the first percussionist to record Bach's complete Violin Sonatas and Partitas on vibes. How do you translate violin bowings and polyphony into four-mallet sticking, pedaling, and dampening?**

That's a good question too! Violin bowing wasn't my main guide. I looked at the slurs and used the pedal to connect the notes so the phrases felt natural, not too short or too long.

For sticking, I tried different combinations to see what worked best. I used all four mallets for chords, but for fast passages, like sixteenth and thirty-second notes, I mostly used mallets 2 and 3, the two inner mallets. With multiple voices, I tried to let the longer notes ring while dampening shorter notes or notes that would clash if left ringing.

I actually wrote very few pedal markings in the score. I trusted my ears more, listening to whether the phrase flowed and sounded right. The pedaling grew naturally out of that.

***Lambada* jumps from Piazzolla tangos on vibes to drum set, and you've written originals like *San José* and *Gasoline Can*. How do you keep both chops up and start new pieces?**

On vibraphone, my background is classical, but I don't really think of myself as strictly a classical or jazz player. I choose music I love, whether it's a classical piece or a song, and arrange it when needed. That makes me want to keep practicing and refining it. I want to play it with depth and make people feel something.

On drum set, I learn a lot from great players. Rich Huang had a big influence on me, and I also watch videos of other legend drummers. I record myself, then watch and listen for what I can improve: accents, clarity, and groove or where I could improve a little more.

For writing or arranging, it often starts with a concert or something my percussion ensemble needs. I explore sounds and ideas that I like. I don't follow a strict framework. I try things and listen to what sounds good to me.

**You founded Cha Cha Percussion in Santa Clara in 2016 and opened your El Camino Real studio in 2020. Why build the program around group ensembles rather than private lessons?**

My first real percussion experience was an ensemble concert, and it blew me away. The energy of many players working together stayed with me. That's why I build programs around ensembles, not just private lessons. I want kids to grow from kindergarten to high school with a shared, lasting musical memory, and adults to reconnect with the joy of music. For me, group learning isn't only about technique, it's also about human connection.

**Through Cha Cha Percussion's Flying Percussion classes and Friends of Children with Special Needs (FCSN), you lead ensembles for students with special needs. How did that begin?**

In 2015, a friend in the South Bay introduced me to FCSN. Some of their students were really into music, and percussion felt like a good fit, so I helped them form an ensemble. That's how it began.

**Between teaching at De Anza College, running Cha Cha Percussion and its summer camp, and freelancing around the Bay Area, what does a normal week look like for you?**

Most of my work is in the afternoons and evenings, so I spend my mornings preparing music materials and exercising. I usually teach percussion ensembles at Cha Cha Percussion in the afternoons and evenings. On Wednesday mornings, I teach at De Anza College, and Saturdays are busy with percussion ensembles all day.

Sundays are my own time. I enjoy my coffee at home, do some cleaning and organizing, and go out for a walk.

**As I head into college to study the music business, I want to learn how musicians build careers that last. What advice would you give a young drummer starting out today?**

I would say, first, think about whether you want to make music your full-time career, and whether you want to focus on performing, teaching, or both. If you're interested in performing, what kind of music do you want to play? Do you see yourself as a soloist or playing in a group? If you enjoy teaching, what age group do you like working with?

College is a great time to explore those questions. Take opportunities to perform, teach, and do internships. You have to try things to find out what feels right for you. By the time you get to graduate school or start working, you'll have a clearer idea of what you want to do. That experience can also make the transition into working in music much smoother.
