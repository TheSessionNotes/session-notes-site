Steven Christopher is a Memphis-raised, LA-based R&B singer and songwriter — warm, vocal-forward records aimed straight at the heartstrings. He's independent, and he does nearly every job his career requires himself, from the writing and production to the visuals and the business.

Along the way, he also engineered one of TikTok's biggest dance trends. In 2022 he built a sped-up remix of Justine Skye's "Collide" that took off, revived a nearly decade-old song commercially, and put his production in front of millions. It's a great story — but it's one chapter in a bigger one about building a career on your own terms.

We talked about all of it: Memphis, his process, the viral moment, and the long list of hats an independent artist has to wear. He was refreshingly plain throughout.

First, here's what he actually sounds like — performing his single "Don't Wait" for On The Radar.

**Let's start with Memphis. What was it like coming up in music there, and how does it compare to Atlanta and LA?**

In Memphis it was really just me building a foundation — influence-wise. Singing in church. Going to the bars and lounges and hearing family sing, that whole thing. Music has always been an undertone of the city. You've got the birth of the blues, the birth of rock and roll, gangster rap — a lot of it pretty much started in Memphis. That's something I didn't even realize growing up, that rock and roll started there. It was just an undertone of life.

Every city has had a little bit of an effect on me. I was already a fan of what Atlanta was doing — Usher, Outkast, Future, who's one of my biggest influences — before I even moved there. Then I came to LA and started putting myself out. LA is cool because there are so many different people here to collaborate with, especially visually. Each city kind of helped mold my taste.

**Memphis doesn't have the same music infrastructure as Atlanta or LA. Did that ever hold you back, or did it even matter?**

Honestly, Memphis is really big for music now. You've got Young Dolph, GloRilla, Key Glock, Pooh Shiesty, NLE Choppa. There's a lot of people coming out of Memphis. So whatever was lacking when I was there is definitely made up for since.

**You've told me before that a lot of your songs get written in the shower. What happens between an idea hitting you and a finished song?**

Yeah, I write a lot of music in the shower. I think water has an effect on creativity.

From there it's usually a bunch of voice notes — or I'll run to my laptop and a mic and put the idea down, just a scratch vocal. Then it's a lot of knob-twisting: adding things, trying things out until it feels right. Drums usually come after I've got a melody idea. It's a lot of playing around with sounds between the point where an idea shows up and where there's a final song.

<div class="statement">Then came the moment everything got louder.</div>

**Let's talk about Collide. Remind me what you actually did, and how it took off.**

I saw it was going crazy — my version of it was going crazy on YouTube and TikTok — but I wasn't getting any credit for it. That didn't feel good. So I was like, alright, what can I do around that to have some impact, or receive some of that?

I started doing reactions to people doing the dance challenges. And then I thought, let's take the sound — I sampled my own voice, pitched it up, and made a beat around it.

**What's been the most frustrating part of the music process, or the business side?**

Honestly, nothing specific. It's all a process. You just have to be patient and do the work. That's what I'm doing.

**You're independent right now. What's behind that — and do you want to stay that way?**

I wouldn't say I want to *stay* independent. I just am, right now. I'm open to both.

My thing is, whoever I work with, I want it built on respect — appreciation of my talent, and my appreciation for whatever it is that they do. It kind of depends. But I'm open.

**What skills did you realize you had to learn as you grew that you had no idea about when you started?**

Everything. I've learned everything, because I do everything.

I didn't know I'd have to become a performer, a producer. Didn't know I'd have to learn to be a writer, an editor, a content creator, an art director, a stylist, a publicist, a booking agent, a music director. All of that — I've learned it, and I'm doing it myself.

**Last one. What advice do you have for anybody starting out or trying to navigate the industry?**

Just focus on being the best you can be, talent-wise — whatever it is you do. Focus on that, and everything else kind of lines up and falls into place. At the end of the day, people will only pay attention if the songs are good. I think the music itself is really good, and that's the only reason people pay any attention.
