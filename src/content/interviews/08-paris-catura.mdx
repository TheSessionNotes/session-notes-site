Paris Catura is a jazz drummer, originally from San Jose and raised in Pleasanton, who moved to Chicago in 2021 knowing no one and built a life in the city's music scene from a blank slate. He plays jazz most of all, but also R&B, neo-soul, funk, and salsa — and as a Filipino American, he carries his heritage into the music, including a Filipino jazz-fusion group called the Philharmonic. This fall he starts a fully funded master's at Northern Illinois University.

What makes his story worth telling isn't a viral moment or a big break. It's the opposite: a case study in consistency. He still goes to three jam sessions a week. He'll tell you plainly that he hasn't "made it," and that he's not sure the feeling of having made it exists. This is a conversation about the unglamorous engine of a music career — and the mindset it takes to keep it running.

**What was it like going from the Bay Area scene to Chicago?**

I moved from Pleasanton to Chicago in 2021 for college, and I didn't know anybody. I was coming in on a blank slate, just kind of risking it. It was during the pandemic, when things were starting to come back, so everybody was applying to every school — all the big names. I actually had trouble getting into some of my safe schools because so many people were auditioning. I was trying to go to LA at first; I had a connection at Long Beach, but there was basically one spot open and a 0.01% chance. Columbia College Chicago gave me a good opportunity, so I took it.

The culture is different here, but honestly, I love it. People are real with you. They're friendly — they've got that Midwestern charm. And the scene is huge. There's a big punk scene, a huge indie scene, an experimental scene, and the jazz scene is really big. When I first got here I was thrown into all of it — people constantly telling me, you've got to come to this show, you've got to hear this. In the Bay, I felt like there honestly wasn't as much going on, at least that I was hearing about. It was almost a culture shock. People are proud here, especially in the music scene.

**You're about to start a fully funded master's at NIU. What made you choose it?**

The jazz program there is highly respected. The teachers are big names — people who've played with a lot of great jazz musicians. And the program focuses on making sure everyone who comes through has a specific goal in mind, so the faculty can actually help you reach it. They make sure you're learning your history and doing things for the right reasons — that you're not just doing it to do it, but because you love it. I have a lot of respect for those guys.

And I got a full ride. I'm going to be a graduate assistant, basically a TA for the grad students. Tuition's paid, I get a stipend, ten hours a week, and I get my master's in two years. I'm very excited.

**You always had the chops. How did school actually refine you?**

I've got to give kudos to my drum professor at Columbia, Charles Heath. He was a huge influence — and not just musically. He'd actually sit us down and talk business. If you want to get out and play gigs, you've got to do this, you've got to do that — marketing yourself, being respectful toward everyone you might get hired by, presenting yourself well. That's something you don't get from most mentors. A lot of teachers keep it surface-level — the classes, the private lessons. He helped me with the business side that not many people talk about, and it reshaped how I thought about the industry and how to actually get around the performing scene here.

Because the whole thing about Chicago is that it's all connections. Having those connections is a big part of the business, and making a good name for yourself is huge.

**So how do you actually get out on the scene? What was getting your first gigs like?**

It's funny — someone else just asked me this. And honestly, I still feel like I'm working on getting out on the scene. It's never like, "I've made it." I'm always thinking, alright, what can I do next to get a gig?

When I was starting out, I was hard on myself. I wasn't getting anything. But I realized it's a mindset thing. You have to believe you're on the path even when you can't see where it leads. So I just kept reaching out to people. The big one is jam sessions — I still go to about three a week, three different ones. That's a huge marketing tool for yourself: you go out, you play, people hear you, and you start meeting people. Someone will say, "Hey, I've got a gig Saturday, you want to play on it?" There we go. I'd do the same thing at shows — introduce myself, say I loved the set, here's my Instagram, let me know if you ever need a sub. Getting those first couple of gigs motivated me, and ever since I got out of college in the summer of 2025, I've just been consistent with it.

**So it's less about luck and more about consistency and work ethic?**

Exactly. My belief is, if you believe you can get where you want to go, you'll get there. If there's ever a doubt in your mind, you're not going to make it. Your mind is such a powerful thing — it gets you through the hard times, the sad times, and it keeps you humble. If I go to a jam and think, "I didn't play well, that was bad," and I keep thinking that, I'll never see my own growth or the opportunities I've been given. There's always some luck involved, sure. But half the battle — if not most of it — is mindset and willpower.

**Your work takes a lot of energy. How do you handle the days when you don't have it — force yourself out, or rest until you're in the right headspace?**

Both, honestly. Sometimes if I don't feel like going to the jam, I force myself, and I get there and have a great time, or someone shows up and I get to play with them. But other times you can't overdo it. I've had to hold myself back from FOMO — the "what if this, what if that." Sometimes you just have to take a break and let your mind and body recover.

That's tied to something personal. I have a skin condition — topical steroid withdrawal. I grew up with eczema, but if you use steroid creams too long your body gets dependent on them, and when you stop, you go through withdrawal. Your whole body flares up, and you end up on a different spectrum than normal eczema, where your body doesn't produce the enzyme it got dependent on. I've been recovering for about two years. A big thing for me was staying happy, motivated, and sane while trying to be in the music industry — which is all about being out in public. I had to get over how I looked, my body looking kind of messed up.

That's why I preach the mental health thing a little heavier. I know how hard it is to deal with the anxiety and stress. Sometimes you have to make sure your choices are in your best interest — not for anyone else. If you're not doing something that makes you happy, you shouldn't be doing it. You should push yourself, but never push yourself over the edge.

**Has pushing over the edge ever cost you on a gig?**

Multiple times. I've been shaking after a gig purely from lack of energy — not eating, not sleeping. I've played gigs on one or two hours of sleep because I wanted everything to be perfect for the next one, and you get behind the kit and think, "Holy shit, I'm tired, I don't even know if I can play what I want to play," because your body doesn't have the rest it needed. It's a learning process. I have late nights, so my sleep schedule gets messed up, and navigating that is its own thing.

**What's the skill you least expected you'd need to grow?**

The ability to say no.

I didn't realize doing a bunch of stuff trains you for that. Early on you want every gig — you're like, I need exposure, I'll do anything. But you get to a point where you can say no: because you need to rest, or because something doesn't feel right, or it's not something you feel confident doing. Being able to say no is a huge thing I've learned over the past two years. (I still do plenty of no-paying gigs, to be clear — for people I love playing with, for the music.)

The other big growth is just going out and listening. Finding the Chicago greats who've been seasoned in the scene forever, and talking to them as people, not celebrities — because everyone, deep down, is just a person. I'll hear someone play something over a tune and think, I've got to steal that, or I've got to ask where that came from. Sometimes it's not even that deep — it's just, how did they make that groove feel so good? Listening to people who make the music feel really good, and trying to learn where they got it, has been huge for me.

**Your family's been a big part of this. How so?**

My parents have been there for everything. They're the first people I go to when I've got something deep to talk about. My mom's a vocalist — she calls herself a "retired vocalist," but how can you be retired if you sing all the time? My dad plays sax, and I've gigged with him. He's silent but supportive — one of those dads who's reserved but you can tell he really cares. He's always checking in: how'd that gig go, you got anything this week, you making enough money? Doing the parent thing, but out of love.

When I was getting ready for college, they told me: if you really want to do this, if you love music, we'll support you all the way — even if it means you're thousands of miles away. They didn't want me moving across the country, but they let me. They've been so supportive through everything, including the TSW recovery. Just a loving family, and if I ever stray, they help me get back on the right path.

**What's a gig that stands out?**

I've got two. This past May I spent a weekend in Detroit playing with a jazz quartet — some of my favorite musicians to play with, guys a few years older than me who feel like big brothers. Getting that call felt like a big break; I was so honored. It was a whole weekend of music: we landed Friday night and went straight to a gig at a hotel downtown, played two hours, went home, woke up, rehearsed, played again, and then the big one — the saxophone player does shows in a park in his hometown every summer. This was a John Coltrane tribute. Coltrane's music is intense, high-energy, hard theory-wise, and it's got a very deep root in Black American music. Being asked to honor that — to emulate Elvin Jones, Coltrane's drummer — was such an honor. You don't get much sleep, but you're playing all day having the time of your life. It wasn't a huge crowd, but everyone there came because they love that music. That's what made it one of my favorites.

The other was at Navy Pier, opening for the great Marquis Hill, alongside names like Bobby Broom, Leonard Simpson, Kassa Overall, and one of Chicago's living jazz legends, Ari Brown — one of the few left from that generation. We played with a phenomenal jazz tap dancer, Jumaane Taylor. It was surreal — playing with people I love, opening for names that big. Two ends of the spectrum: the park gig was emotionally and musically fulfilling; this one was fulfilling because I was opening for people who are really doing the thing.

**Any plans for the future, and any advice for younger drummers coming up?**

The future's unknown, so I'm going with the flow — seeing where school takes me. Beyond that, just keep doing the thing, keep pushing, keep reaching for more, and make sure you're doing it for the right reasons: honoring the history, the music, the space. I'll keep going to jams at least three times a week even if I have a full-time job or start teaching, because that's what you've got to do. And if I ever make it big, I'll still go — it's about paying it forward. Once you get to a certain point, you're not better than anyone else. You started where everyone else started. You've got to pay your dues.

As for advice: train your mind and train your body. Train your body for the physical part — the chops, the performing. But train your mind to love learning, and to have respect for everyone around you, especially yourself. And pay it forward. That's all I can say. Never stop believing.
